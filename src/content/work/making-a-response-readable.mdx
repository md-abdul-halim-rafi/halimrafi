<Lede>An answer that arrives all at once is easy to read. An answer that arrives in pieces has to be readable at every point in between. None of that is model quality. It is a set of choices about what to show and when, and somebody on the product side has to make them.</Lede>

## The problem

Streaming renders whatever has arrived, the moment it arrives. That is the honest
default, and it produces three specific failures. All three are visible below if
you switch the prototype to Raw arrival.

- A sentence assembles a fragment at a time, so the reader is chasing a moving
  target instead of reading.
- Formatting crosses chunk boundaries. The stream sends `including their **`
  while the closing marker is still on its way, and the reader sees the syntax.
- A tool result has nowhere to go until it arrives. When it lands it inserts
  itself and pushes everything after it down the page, usually just as the reader
  reaches the line below.

The third one is the worst, because it punishes the reader for reading.

## The decision

Show text at the size of a readable unit, and hold space for anything that has
been asked for but has not come back yet.

That is three rules, all of them visible in Considered reveal:

1. A paragraph appears once it is closed, and a list item once its line lands.
   The reader gets whole thoughts instead of a growing edge of text.
2. Markup that has opened and not closed is withheld along with the text after
   it, so the syntax never reaches the screen. This is a separate pass from the
   buffering: waiting for whole words would not do it.
3. The record's slot is created when the tool is called rather than when it
   answers, and it is sized by the record it is waiting for, so the arrival moves
   nothing below it. That is exact here because the fixture is scripted and the
   finished record can be measured in advance; a real result of unknown size
   would need a fixed slot height or a different treatment.

This is a decision rather than a detail because of what is and is not ours to
choose. We do not choose when the pieces arrive: the model sets its own pace and
the tool takes as long as it takes. The only thing we choose is when to show what
has already arrived.

<Disclosure summary="Implementation detail">

Two choices carry most of the weight.

**The view is derived from elapsed time.** Rather than appending to a buffer as
events arrive, the renderer takes the event list, an elapsed value and a mode,
and computes the entire visible state from them. Playback is then moving a
number. Pause stops moving it, replaying sets it to zero, and switching modes
recomputes from the same events, so the two sides of a comparison cannot drift
apart or mix runs.

The same elapsed value always produces the same output. The tests sample the
timeline out of order and after a rewind and check that no text repeats, which
covers the cases that would show duplication in use. That is evidence under
those samples, not a proof about every input.

**Markup is tracked separately from buffering.** A small pass over the
accumulated text finds delimiters that have opened and not closed, and withholds
from the opening marker onward. It handles `**` and backticks, which is what this
fixture contains. It is not a general parser and does not attempt nesting, links
or code fences.

**The waiting slot is sized by the thing it is waiting for.** The placeholder
does not take its height from its own text. The finished record is rendered
underneath it and made invisible, so the space held is the space the record will
need. This is exact here only because the fixture is scripted and that record can
be measured in advance. A real result of unknown size would need a fixed slot
height or a different treatment.

The 2.4 second point at which the slot admits it is late is a setting in this
demo, chosen so the behaviour is visible in a short demonstration.

</Disclosure>

## An alternative

The obvious middle ground is word-level reveal: hold each word until it is
complete, then show it. It is cheaper than what I built, so it deserves a real
answer.

Word-level reveal fixes half the problem. Holding a word until it is whole
removes half-words at almost no cost in wait. It does nothing about formatting,
because buffering and markup are separate concerns: a bold span can open in one
word and close several words later, so a reveal that waits for whole words still
shows a complete, unclosed `**`. Whatever the buffering unit, something has to
track which markers are open and withhold from the opening marker onward.

What word-level reveal does not fix is reading. A line that grows word by word
keeps moving its own right edge, and the eye re-anchors on every arrival. That
suits someone waiting to see whether anything is happening. It works against
someone trying to take in an answer.

Paragraph-level reveal gives up that sense of motion for a block that holds
still. In this scripted demo the cost is **520 milliseconds to first visible
text**, measured from the start of playback, against zero for raw arrival. Those
are properties of the fixture, not of any deployed system.

The argument for paying it is that a finished sentence is easier to follow than a
growing one. That is a design hypothesis. Nothing here measures it, and it will
not hold equally for every reader, connection or kind of content.

## The tradeoff

Considered reveal is slower to first visible text, and that is the price of the
whole approach. The 520ms above is small only because this fixture's first
paragraph is short. The cost scales with the length of the unit being held, and
that is where the approach stops being the right call:

- **Long paragraphs.** Holding a six-sentence paragraph could mean seconds of
  nothing on screen, and a long enough pause reads as a failure rather than as
  care. Grouping at sentence boundaries inside long paragraphs would help. This
  prototype does not do it.
- **Short lookups.** When someone asks a question with a one-line answer, they
  want to see it start. The grouping is just delay.
- **Code and tables.** Neither approach works. Partially rendered code reads as
  complete and is wrong, so those blocks want all or nothing.
- **Bursty connections.** When chunks arrive in clumps, the clump boundary and
  the paragraph boundary tend to coincide and the distinction stops earning its
  complexity.

So this is a reasonable default for answers that are mostly prose and a few
sentences long, which is what this product produces. It is not a rule about
streaming interfaces in general, and I would not apply it to a terminal or a
code assistant.

What is on this page is a prototype rather than shipped software: a fixed local
script, no model behind it, and no measurements in it.

<Disclosure summary="How this prototype was checked">

Automated, with `pnpm test`:

- Both scenarios keep their events in chronological order, and the delayed
  scenario differs from the normal one by exactly one value.
- Raw arrival exposes the unclosed bold span while it is open. Considered reveal
  does not.
- A paragraph is withheld until it closes, and released once the stream settles.
- The projection is a pure function of elapsed time, and sampling out of order
  never duplicates text.
- A late result does not hold back the prose or the provenance line around it,
  and it resolves into its original position.
- Both modes finish at the same answer.
- The 520ms figure quoted above is read from the fixture, so the prose cannot
  drift from the prototype.

By hand in a browser: play, pause, resume, replay after completion, reset,
switching modes and scenarios mid-run, keyboard traversal of every control, and
the completed transcript with JavaScript turned off.

</Disclosure>
